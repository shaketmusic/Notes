How do we here stereo? Is it about volume, or about time? Alan Blumlein pioneered the method we use today, which is only a matter of **volume**.

- When and audio source is panned to the **center**, it comes out equally out of both sides, exactly the same.

- When an audio source is hard panned either left or right, one speaker would be muted. Because the signal gets doubled in the center, audio gear manufacturers design the gear so that the audio gets turned down (by -3dB/-6dB/-4.5dB) on the center in the console or DAW, to keep the output the same – that is called the **pan law**.

**See also:** [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=spaqBr-cCFw](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=spaqBr-cCFw)

### Panning Stereo Tracks
In ProTools, stereo tracks have two panning controls. You can have that in Ableton too by right clicking on the panner and choosing “**select split stereo pan mode**”


### Mono to Stereo
Don’t try to make a mono track stereo by copying the same audio to a different channel and panning hard left and right. It will only make it louder.

To actually make it wider we use **doubling** (recording two takes), or – copy the original track except change the pitch, the timing and timber a bit by using effects. That would make it wide.

If you’re not sure if a track is two monos that are exactly the same or stereo, you can check by flipping the faze of one of the channels. In Pro tools – pan both left and right to the center, put the **trim** plugin and flip the faze of one of the channel. In Ableton’s utility or Logic’s gain click on mono and flip one of the channels. 

**Check every track you’re not sure about.** You can do this for stereo mixes to analyze what’s panned in the track. You can learn how loud a reverb is, and what its tone is. You might discover, for example, that when you want a dry mono main vocal, usually producers put a really quiet reverb to add a little depth.
